Akshat Sharma is a fourth-year BS–MS dual-degree student and Reliance Foundation Scholar at the Indian Institute of Science Education and Research (IISER) Kolkata. His research operates at the intersection of soft matter, biophysics, statistical mechanics, and information theory. The overarching objective of his work is to understand the fundamental physical principles that govern self-assembly, systemic complexity, and the emergence of living matter from inanimate components.

His academic trajectory is grounded in experimental chemistry, beginning with the synthesis and study of giant polyoxometalate (POM) clusters. From this experimental foundation, his focus has evolved toward developing theoretical and mathematical frameworks capable of describing molecular and macroscopic organization across diverse physical systems. Rather than viewing chemical systems in isolation, he utilizes them as experimentally accessible model platforms to uncover universal organizational rules.

His recent theoretical work focuses on quantifying complexity and structural organization, resulting in two primary contributions:

The Quantum Assembly Index (QAI): An information-theoretic descriptor of molecular organization. This metric is derived directly from quantum electron density topology using the Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules (QTAIM), extending structural assembly concepts into a strictly quantum-mechanical framework.

The Core–Halo Theorem: An analytical result within generalized statistical complexity. This theorem provides a mathematical proof that maximally complex probability distributions universally organize into a core–halo structural configuration.

To anchor his theoretical models in physical reality, he maintains a rigorous methodological toolkit that bridges physical and computational chemistry. His technical expertise encompasses molecular dynamics, density functional theory (DFT), and single-crystal X-ray crystallography, alongside the practical synthesis and solid-state characterization of complex inorganic materials.

Currently, his research applies these combined theoretical and computational approaches to non-equilibrium thermodynamics and active matter. By integrating information-theoretic measures with statistical mechanics, he aims to build comprehensive physical models that explain how chemical architectures transition into complex, organized systems.
